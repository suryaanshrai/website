If you spend enough time staring at a code editor, your sense of physical scale and that touch with the natural world disorients. And that requires a reset.

So, on a random Wednesday night, when my younger brother was back home for his vacation, we packed our bags, boarded an overnight bus from Delhi, and decided to press the "Reset Your Routine" button of our lives with an escapade so good that even two well planned days were more than enough to elevate the baseline of experience of my life in that the days that followed always gave me an itch to just go out and experience something similar again. 

*The destination?* 

**Dharamshala**

###  The Zipline Warm-Up

We reached there on Thursday morning, the 17th, with a bit of post-overnight-bus grogginess. Naturally, the best way to cure a lack of sleep is to throw yourself off a cliff.

Before we even started the actual trek, I ended up at a ziplining spot (had seen it on Google maps the previous day so just had it in the checklist to do it). AND, as if flying over a gorge on a wire wasn't enough, they also had "zipline cycling." AND YES, my happy inner me who had went out for an adventure after quite a while, excitedly hopped on that tireless cycle hooked on a couple of thin metal wires. And yes, pedaling a literal bicycle on a thin metal rope suspended over a massive drop gives you one very specific, terrifying moment. When that bike bends a bit on one side and the instructor had told you to just not press the brake and keep pedaling, and you right on the middle of ravine, looking at the empty air beneath your tires (or just metal rings TBH), wondering if you should have been active and controlled your weight from growing this much, that moment my dear friend, is entirely worth it.

### Philosophizing: The favorite activity of the ones who walk
To be completely honest: my lifestyle lately has been embarrassingly sedentary. Like I barely move, mostly just sit on my desk, and even for entertainment I play video games. I know its something to work upon, but my point right now is, that even with such a sedentary lifestyle, I was able to do the trek. I won't say that it was effortless, but it was not as hard as I had thought. There were also many people who were a bit aged, some parents with their kids in their 40s probably, a group of ladies probably in their mid-30s (who trekked with us for a while because my brother had a good thing going with his choice of music that day, mostly starring Ritviz, but our DJ was doing a good job for the trek), and many others who did not seemed like the healthiest most fit people, and yet were enjoying the trek fairly.

Which spiraled my mind down to yet another thought as we were closing in towards the summit where our camp was supposed to be, *the capabilities of the human body*. Its as if I had almost forgotten what my body is capable of. It was easily over a 20-25k step trek and steps that i had done today. Mostly steep climb. And though I was tired, I knew that I could still do a few sets of a few pushups on the top, and still walk more at a relaxed pace. And when I saw where I was in the morning and where I was now, the sheer gap in the place, the fact that not even our everyday vehicles can do this trek (at least on the path that I took). And all this made me feel that I am not pushing and maintaining my body to the full extent of what it is capable of. 

All the people running these makeshift cafes had to do this trek almost everyday. The short supply of fresh water, ration, and other supplies, etc. They were doing it everyday. Then I started thinking of people who do it an even more extreme levels, think of men of the Indian Army protecting the Siachen glacier, people climbing on Mount Everest, rock climbing with bare safety equipment. Then there are the ones exploring the ocean, and I know for a fact that it is a completely different game. And then there's the league of Armstrong and the likes, humans who went to the moon. Humans who pushed boundaries. Humans who explored. These are the kind of people who makes us human. Survival for us is to explore and conquer that which is out there, unknown and unexplored by anyone. Maybe that is the reason why we also are the apex predator, because its not just the prey that we hunt, we live by exploration. The thoughts, understanding and everything follows, First, is the will, the passion, the drive, the curiosity, the hunger, to know learn and get into that which is untouched yet. Obviously there are multiple facets to it. But these were the kinds of thoughts and feelings I was experiencing in those moments there. This was totally something that had refreshed me. Maybe it was me being cutoff from the screens for a while, the getting away from the constant rush of everything that is now everyday life. The slowing down, the seeing what is there, and how much there is more to see. Maybe it won't be new place that I go to in terms of unexplored places, maybe it will be a tourist spot packed with a lot of other humans and nothing new for the rest of the world, but for it will be something that I have explored, it would be a new place, a new experience, a new exploration and a new wonder that I will have, cherish and carry with me. These feelings had made one thing sure for me, I need more experiences like this, these feelings that made me feel so alive and full of life. 

Or maybe it was just the experience paired with the audiobook of Sapiens by Yuval Noah Harari that I was listening to on the way here. 

But whatever it was, it had revived me.  

And then, I reached the summit.

Standing in front of the Dhauladhar range is a humbling, almost physical sensation. It isn't just about the massive verticality of the peaks; it’s the silence. It’s not the empty silence of a quiet room, but a heavy, ancient silence that seems to absorb the sound of everything else.


I stood there, looking all the way back down at the valley. The bustling, noisy town we had started from in the morning had been reduced to a tiny, quiet cluster of pixel-sized buildings.

It sounds incredibly basic, but physically experiencing that delta—the massive distance between your 8:00 AM self and your 3:00 PM self—does something to your brain.



It’s easy to feel insignificant in front of a mountain. But as the physical exhaustion cleared, that feeling of smallness turned into a weird, roaring sense of human pride. I looked at my hands, felt my chest rising and falling, and realized: _I am built from the exact same evolutionary blueprint as the people who do this every day. We are the same ridiculously adaptable species that looked at Mount Everest and decided to climb it. The same species that tamed fire, calculated trajectories to land on the Moon, and built microscopic transistors._

### 03:45 AM: A Dad's Geography Lesson

The ultimate perspective check came in the dead of night.

My dad is a geography teacher. Growing up, he always used to tell me that the sky is at its darkest and clearest around $3:45\text{ AM}$, making it the absolute prime window for stargazing.

So, at exactly $3:45\text{ AM}$, I unzipped the tent and stepped out into the freezing cold. The mountain air felt like needles in my lungs.

Because everyone in the camp had kept their lights off, the entire ridge was cast in absolute, pitch-black darkness. I tilted my head back, and there it was.

For the first time in my life, I saw the Milky Way with my own naked eyes.

It takes a minute or two for your eyes to adjust to that kind of dark. It wasn’t the vibrant, hyper-saturated purple and blue you see in high-exposure Hubble photographs, but a ghostly, faint, luminous lane of cosmic dust cutting silently across the black canvas. I ended up taking a photo a bit later when the light had shifted slightly, so the shot is a bit faint, but the mental image is permanently burned in.

At that moment, the scales of my world aligned:

- The physical kilometers of the steep mountain climb.
    
- The light-years of the cosmic dust lane overhead.
    

We occupy a very specific, beautiful middle ground in this universe. We are small enough to struggle up a hill, but conscious enough to look up at the stars and recognize our own galaxy.

### Descent? Jawan Aur Uske Josh ka Rutba Kabhi Kam Nahi Hota

We woke up at $6:30\text{ AM}$ on the 18th to hot tea and breakfast, and by $7:00\text{ AM}$, we were already on our way down.

We reached the trailhead at Bhagsu Nag around 1:00 PM. Now, if you’ve ever been to Dharamshala, you know the traffic near the trailheads can be a absolute nightmare, and the local cab drivers quote prices that assume you’re a traveling tech billionaire.

This is where my younger brother’s superb "josh" and high-energy enthusiasm kicked in. Instead of waiting for a cab or paying through our noses, he convinced me that we should just walk the remaining $2\text{ km}$ back to Zostel Dharamshala on foot.

Honestly? It was a genius move. It only took us about 30 to 40 minutes, bypassed the bumper-to-bumper traffic entirely, saved us a chunk of change, and gave us a nice transition back into civilization.

### Monks, Murals, and Mindful Lotuses

After dropping our bags and resting for a bit, we took a leisurely stroll around Dharamshala.

We visited the local Buddhist temple—an incredibly peaceful, quiet sanctuary. But the highlight of the afternoon happened just outside. As we passed a roadside cafe, I got a glimpse of a group of Buddhist monks in deep crimson robes huddled over a carrom board inside.

There was something so wonderfully human about it. These are people who dedicate their entire lives to spiritual discipline and the pursuit of cosmic mindfulness, yet in that moment, their entire universe had shrunk to the position of a plastic striker and a pink wooden queen. It was an instant serotonin boost.

Further down the street, I saw a local lady painting directly in front of her shop.

I wanted to pick up some souvenirs for my parents, so I stopped by to make some small talk. I asked her about her work, and she gladly showed me all of her paintings. One in particular stood out: a massive painting of a lotus, easily the size of a large television screen (if not bigger). The level of detail was incredibly meticulous.

When I asked her about it, she smiled and told me that her entire family does this, and that painting these intricate designs "relaxes the mind." When she told me the price, it was definitely on the higher side—but looking closely at the sheer hours of focus woven into the canvas, it was completely obvious why. It wasn't just art; it was a physical manifestation of patience. I asked her if I could take a picture of her while she painted, and she kindly agreed.

### Wrapping Up at Labooz

To wrap up our final night, my brother and I went cafe-hopping. We ended up at Labooz Bar and Cafe, which was an absolute vibe. Great food, great live music, incredibly friendly staff, and stellar views of the valley lighting up as the sun went down.

On the 19th, we boarded our bus back to Delhi.

Looking back at the screen of my phone as we rolled down the highway, the world didn't feel quite as small anymore. We had climbed a giant, stood under a galaxy, and shared a quiet moment with the people who call the mountains home. Not bad for a long weekend.
